*Ruth Greenwood is the director of the Election Law Clinic at Harvard Law School.*

Seven years ago, I lost a redistricting case in the Supreme Court. I didn’t just lose — the court ruled that no one could ever bring a claim like mine to federal courts again. That’s about as big a loss as you can get. Like any good sports player, I have spent the past seven years being salty at the refs. But it’s time to admit the dice were loaded from the start. We need to play a new game.

That case was [Rucho v. Common Cause](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/rucho-v-common-cause-2/___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6NzphYmQwOjU0YmY2ZWEyOGZlNjVjMjM3MGQ3NDYyOTRlY2NmYTc0YjMwOTdjZjU3Y2I2YzhiZTI3OGNhMzNlYTU2OWU4NWI6aDpUOkY): I represented the League of Women Voters of North Carolina and individual voters challenging congressional district lines drawn to guarantee Republicans an overwhelming advantage. Under those maps, despite the state’s votes [splitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_North_Carolina) almost evenly between the two parties, Republicans captured 10 of North Carolina’s 13 congressional seats in 2018, to just three for Democrats. The outcome was preordained before a single vote was cast.

Sadly, this reality is a feature, not a bug, when it comes to a single-member district system. A single-member district is an area that elects just one person to represent it, so whichever party wins a majority of the vote gets 100 percent of the representation, and the other party gets nothing. Draw the lines one way, and Republicans get shut out; draw them another, and Democrats do. It doesn’t matter who draws the map — a judge, a computer, a bipartisan commission — because single-member districts turn every election into a zero-sum fight over where to put a single line.

There’s a fix, and I’ve lived under it. I grew up and first voted in Australia, [where the Senate](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://www.aph.gov.au/About_Parliament/Senate/Practice_and_Procedure/Senate_Briefs/Brief01___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6NzoyNWExOmI3NjNjZjE0ZWU3YjU4YzczNDg2NGVhNmJjZTEyZDZlNmY5OGQzYzI2NzE1YmM0N2ZlOWQwNDViNzc3ODg3NGU6aDpUOkY) is elected using a form of proportional representation. At their core, proportional systems use multimember districts to translate votes into seats proportionally. A majority still elects the majority, but the minority has a real shot of gaining representation. If one party wins 40 percent of the vote, it still gets 40 percent of the seats. By tying seats to votes, gerrymandering is stripped of its effectiveness, because the minority party can still pick up wins. According to a [new report](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://protectdemocracy.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/When-Maps-Dont-Matter.pdf___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6Nzo5MjU1OjU2YmRmMjVmZDEyOWQ0NWYzZTllNDc4MmVhMWJkNDg2MWI0OTg5ZjkwZDgwMzRkYjM4OTM3ZWQ1Zjk1Mjg4YTA6aDpUOkY) by the nonpartisan group Protect Democracy, under proportional representation, “\[t\]ry as they might, gerrymanderers will find it prohibitively difficult — often, impossible — to draw any group of voters out of representation.”

Until the game changes, gerrymandering will only escalate. My colleague Paul Smith in 2017 [warned the Supreme Court](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://www.supremecourt.gov/oral_arguments/argument_transcripts/2017/16-1161_mjn0.pdf___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6Nzo0ZDE5OmZhOTQ3NGJhMDhiNWZkNGJiNWY4NmZmN2Q5MTU0ZDk2ZGI3MDcwMmYwZDMxY2FhYzdhYTJmM2VhYWI0MjJjNmE6aDpUOkY) (while working with me on another case that we also lost, but who’s counting), that if the justices refused to police partisan gerrymandering, the next decade would bring “a festival of copycat gerrymandering the likes of which this country has never seen.” Even he didn’t see what was to come.

In the past year, states have [drawn and redrawn](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/us/politics/states-gerrymandering-redistricting-maps.html___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6NzplMGI1OjU4MDc1MjFjNWYyNDMxYmY4YWI3MDZiNWE1MDQzZWEzM2EzNWEyNTcwMzRmYTU0NmEzY2U2MmRiNjM3N2I0NDQ6aDpUOkY) their congressional district lines, each time seeking out greater and greater advantage for one of the major parties. This doesn’t just result in a huge skew in favor of certain parties ([Republicans in Texas](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://planscore.org/plan.html?20260701T174310.007254951Z___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6NzphOGU4OjY1NTkxZmU2YzcxMjIwMGU5ZmMwZTRiMTBkNzUyNmU4NTU0MjBkOTM3ODdiYjE4NTQ4YzM3Nzg0ZjJiZjA1ZGE6aDpUOkY), [Democrats in California](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://planscore.org/plan.html?20260625T222246.904713690Z___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6NzplNGNjOjg1YjczNjExZjY5NDM1NjRmMTBmZGVjNjZjNDYwODk2ODFlZDZmMjI4ZjhmNmRiMDBlMjY1ZjJlOWRjZDYzYjE6aDpUOkY)), it means that we already know who will win almost every House race in 2026 — only 4 percent of congressional races are [considered competitive](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://www.270towin.com/2026-house-election/cook-political-report-2026-house-ratings___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6Nzo2ZTc0OjA2ODc2ODRiNGRhNDMzZGVhZDIxYTczZmFiNmUwMWY5OTk2YzJkZjExNTAzZmZhMjg1Njc0NDFjZWZhOTNkNDI6aDpUOkY). Safe seats don’t just suppress competition — they reward polarization, because the only election that decides the outcome is the primary.

I’ve spent years trying to fix this from inside the system: challenging partisan gerrymandering in a [state court](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://www.hlselectionlaw.org/clarke___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6NzphYTlkOmY2MmQ0ZGU1NTA4N2Y1M2YzNDRmMjJhNDQ3ZTZlMTM0MjgzYjEwYTQ0MzAwNjQ1NjhiN2UyZWY5NDViNDIyMDM6aDpUOkY), building coalitions for independent redistricting commissions [with limited success](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/ohio-politicians-try-deceive-voters-about-anti-gerrymandering-ballot___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6NzowYjk2Ojc2NDBiNDYyZGZkN2YxMDFhN2ZhYzYzNmM2N2I2MmRkZWY1OWFlZGEzMDUxNWRjMDc0N2QxOTY1NTAxM2JhYTk6aDpUOkY), even helping to build a [website to visualize](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://planscore.org/%23!predict0-ushouse___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6NzpiNWVkOjg0NWYzNTc5NjIzN2E3Mjc5ZjA5Zjk3YTY5MjJlYWNlY2JiMzg2ZTEyZTM2ZjMwYjM2OWQ2NTgxYzljZTQxMTE6aDpUOkY) just how skewed every map in the country is. Some of it helped, but nothing has stopped the inevitable tide of politicians seeking to lock in their power by gerrymandering, while voters lose out.

Then came the death blow. A few weeks ago, the Supreme Court decided the [Louisiana v. Callais](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-109_21o3.pdf___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6NzoxNjFlOjZiYWRjMWY3ZTY4ZGMyNjdmY2QxMDdhZmUxNzQzZDcyYWYxZTZjMzJkNDc1YWNiMGUyZGZkMGJjOGY0ZDFjNzI6aDpUOkY) case and gutted what remained of the Voting Rights Act’s protections against racial vote dilution. States can now draw maps that erase the political voice of entire communities of color — in Mississippi, for instance, Black residents are 37 percent of the voting-age population, yet a map could plausibly leave them unable to elect a single candidate of their choice to Congress. For the first time since Jim Crow, we’re facing elections where Black, Latino, Asian American, and Native voters could be shut out of representation, entirely by design. This isn’t what a multiracial democracy looks like.

The proportional voting fix could work for the US House, and Congress doesn’t need a constitutional amendment to do it. Single-member districts for the House aren’t required by the Constitution — they’re required by a single [federal statute](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/2/2c___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6NzpiMTExOjk0ZmFmMWFlOTFjMDY3ZjcyMjUyOTgxZWUzNWQzNDY4YWY5ZjFmNmRmMDU0ODZhMTg2MTZhYTk2NzE3MzI3MDE6aDpUOkY) passed in 1967. Amend that one law to let states elect their House delegations through a proportional system and gerrymandering stops being mathematically feasible; mapmakers can move the lines all they want, but the votes still follow the voters. Proportional representation is also race-neutral so it won’t fall foul of the new rules the Supreme Court has made about voting rights and race-neutrality.

Unlike other Australian favorites — for example, Vegemite, which I will defend to the hilt — proportional representation is something Americans can, and are,[warming to](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/10/nx-s1-5920312/proportional-representation-poll). For those of us who study the issue, there is also some broad agreement. This summer, over 500 political scientists and other scholars [signed a letter](https://protect.checkpoint.com/v2/r01/___https://medium.com/_@scholars-redistricting-reform/letter-to-congress-end-the-redistricting-wars-by-adopting-proportional-representation-098001be937c___.YzJ1OmJvc3Rvbmdsb2JlMTpjOmc6YmE0NGJkZDAxY2EwYWY1YmUxOTZlMTdkYzMyOWZkMGU6Nzo1MjBlOjFiY2I2OWQyZTljMDMzODFlMGQ3NDkzZTZhNzI2ZjNiNDVkN2M4ZjUwYTk5MjljNjY3M2EzNTMyNGNmOGE4ZjE6aDpUOkY) urging Congress to adopt proportional representation as an end to the redistricting wars (disclosure: I’m one of the signatories).

I’ve spent my career fighting for the right of every voter to have a real say in how they’re governed. The Supreme Court has now closed off nearly every tool I had to do that through the courts. So I’m done blaming the refs. It’s time for a system where our multiracial democracy is protected, no matter who umpires the game.
